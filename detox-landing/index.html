How the slot machine got into your pocket

Your lack of focus is not a character flaw. It is the intended output of about ninety years of behavioral research, compressed into a glass rectangle and handed to you for free.

By the Unscroll Desk | 14 min read

In 1931, a graduate student at Harvard built a box. Inside it, a rat pressed a lever and food appeared. B.F. Skinner expected the rat to learn the rhythm — press, eat, press, eat — and settle into something like boredom. Then his pellet dispenser jammed. Rewards started arriving at random. And the rat, deprived of any pattern to rely on, began pressing the lever far more often than before.

Skinner had stumbled onto the variable-ratio reinforcement schedule: the discovery that unpredictable rewards produce more compulsive behavior than predictable ones. It is the single most reliable finding in the history of behavioral psychology. It is also the reason you reach for your phone before you have decided to.

I.  The price of free

The economist Herbert Simon saw the shape of the problem two decades before the web existed. If information is abundant, he argued, then the scarce resource is not information — it is the human capacity to attend to it. Any market built on abundant information will therefore become a market in attention. He wrote that in a conference paper. Nobody built a business model on it for thirty years.

Then, in 2000, the dot-com crash took the banner ad down with it. The industry's answer was not to charge users money. It was to get far better at knowing them. Targeting replaced reach. And once the unit of sale became a precisely-identified human being rather than a vague audience, the incentive changed permanently: every additional minute a person spent on a page became directly, measurably, saleable.

This is the part that gets flattened in most retellings. No one sat in a room and decided to make a billion people anxious. What happened is duller and worse: thousands of smart people ran millions of A/B tests, each one optimizing a single number, and the number they were optimizing was time on site. Compulsion was never the goal. It was simply what won the tests.

II.  Four inventions

Four design decisions, shipped between 2006 and 2013, did most of the damage. None of them looked like much at the time.

The infinite scroll arrived in 2006, sketched by a designer named Aza Raskin who wanted to spare people the friction of clicking “next page.” It worked. It also removed the only natural stopping cue the web had. A page that ends gives you a moment to decide whether to continue. A page that never ends never asks.

The News Feed followed weeks later — an algorithmically ranked, continuously refreshed river of other people's lives. Users revolted; hundreds of thousands signed petitions against it. Engagement doubled anyway. That gap, between what people said they wanted and what they measurably did, became the industry's permanent justification for ignoring the first number.

Pull-to-refresh shipped in 2009 in a small Twitter client. You drag the screen down, release, and wait to see what the machine gives you. It is, gesturally and psychologically, the arm of a slot machine. Its own inventor has since called it exactly that.

The push notification completed the circuit. Until 2009, you had to decide to open the app. After 2009, the app could decide to open you — and, within a few years, could learn the precise hour of your precise day at which you were most likely to be susceptible.

III.  The streak, and the shape of a modern habit

By 2015 the loop was closed and the industry moved on to a subtler problem: not how to capture attention, but how to make its absence painful. Snapchat's streak counter is the cleanest example ever shipped — a number that only ever goes up, that two people maintain together, and that disappears forever if either one misses a day.

What makes it remarkable is that it contains no content. There is nothing to read, nothing to watch, nothing of value transferred. It is pure loss aversion, rendered as a small orange flame, and it reliably pulled teenagers back to an app they had otherwise stopped enjoying.

IV.  Why willpower is the wrong tool

The standard advice — use less, be present, try harder — fails for a structural reason. You are not competing against your own weakness. You are competing against an adaptive system, running continuous experiments, with a feedback loop measured in milliseconds and a budget measured in billions.
