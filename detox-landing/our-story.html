A note from the founders

We built a wall.
People climbed it.

Two years and four hardware prototypes taught us something we did not want to learn: you cannot out-engineer an attention monopoly with a single object.

By [Founder Name] | January 2026

Our first product was a piece of folded steel. You clipped it over your phone's screen, and to see anything you had to physically slide a shutter open — a deliberate, two-handed, slightly embarrassing motion. The idea was friction. Make the reach cost something, and the reach gets rarer.

It worked, for about nine days. We know, because we shipped it to 140 beta testers and asked them every evening. Week one, screen time dropped by a third. Week two, it was halfway back. By week four, most people had stopped using the shutter entirely, and a handful had done something we found genuinely clarifying: they left it open permanently, like a drawbridge nobody bothers to raise.

So we built a second version. Then a third — a charging dock with a timed lock, which one tester defeated with a butter knife and another defeated by buying a second phone. The fourth was software-assisted and very clever, and the pattern held exactly: a sharp drop, a slow return, a quiet abandonment.

The uncomfortable conclusion

We tried to build a physical solution to get people off their screens, and we realized something uncomfortable: no single product can cure an attention monopoly. The thing we were fighting is not one app or one gesture. It is thousands of engineers running continuous experiments across a dozen surfaces, with your own habits as the training data. A shutter closes one door in a building with no walls.

Worse, the barrier model has a design flaw baked into its premise. It assumes the problem is access. It isn't. The problem is that the phone has quietly absorbed every job your attention used to do — boredom management, social reassurance, transitions between tasks, the ten seconds of waiting in a queue. Take the phone away and those jobs don't disappear. They just go unmet, which is unpleasant, which is why the shutter stays open by week four.

People don't just need a barrier. They need a framework. An order of operations. A sense of which lever to pull first, what to expect when they pull it, and what to put in the space it opens up. That is not a product feature. That is teaching.

Four things the prototypes taught us

  1. 01 Friction decays. Any obstacle you can defeat the same way twice stops being an obstacle. Habits route around static barriers in roughly two weeks.
  2. 02 Removal needs replacement. Every hour you subtract has to be spent on something. Detoxes that skip this step fail quietly and blame the person.
  3. 03 The trigger matters more than the app. Deleting one app moves the behavior next door. Disarming the trigger — the notification, the unlock reflex, the bedside charger — moves all of them at once.
  4. 04 No one is loyal to a tool. They are loyal to a result. Which is why we stopped selling ours.

So we became agnostic

Unscroll is a newsletter and a toolkit, and deliberately not a device. We have no hardware to protect and no engagement metric to defend, which means we can tell you plainly when the best answer is a free iOS setting, a $4 app we did not build, a kitchen drawer, or nothing at all.

Each week you get one issue: the mechanics of a specific attention trap, explained the way a journalist would explain a market — then the exact counter-moves, with the settings, scripts, and tools laid out so you can run them that evening. Over a few months it accumulates into something a product never gave our testers: a framework they could adapt when the platforms inevitably changed the rules.

We still have a box of steel shutters in the office. Occasionally someone asks to borrow one. We let them — they do work, for about nine days. Now we can also tell them what to do on day ten.
