Week 3 is where the noise starts to clear

By FSN Desk

Every year the same thing happens around now. Two weeks of box scores land, the standings look nothing like the draft board, and half the league starts trading on a sample that cannot support the weight.

Some of what you are looking at is real. Most of it is not. The difference is almost always the same, and it is not subtle once you know where to look.

Usage is signal. Efficiency is noise.

Two games is already enough to see how a coaching staff intends to use somebody. It is nowhere near enough to learn how good he is going to be at it.

Snap share, route participation, carries inside the ten, target share when the game is close: these stabilize fast, because they are decisions rather than outcomes. Somebody chose them. They will keep choosing them until they have a reason not to.

Yards per carry, touchdown rate, catch rate on contested balls: these do not stabilize for months. A back can run for six yards a carry across two weeks and tell you almost nothing about week ten.

So the question to ask about Bijan Robinson, or any back you are trying to value right now, is not what his average looks like. It is whether he is on the field on third down and whether he is the one carrying it from the two. If both are true, the production is coming whether or not it has arrived. If neither is true, a good two weeks is a good two weeks and not a role.

The trap is a good player in a bad situation

The hardest roster call in September is the talented player whose usage says something his box score does not.

This is where target share earns its reputation. A receiver commanding a real share of a competent passing game is the most durable thing in this format, because it survives bad weeks. Puka Nacua is the archetype: the value is in how often the ball is thrown at him, not in what any single Sunday did with it. If the share is there, sit on it. Volume regresses toward production far more reliably than production regresses toward volume.

The inverse is the one that costs people their season. A player scoring well on usage that is not his: a back getting goal line work only because somebody else is hurt, a receiver running a full route tree while the depth chart sorts itself out. That production is real and it is also borrowed.

Tight end is its own problem

Do not evaluate tight ends on the same clock as everyone else.

The position takes longer to sort out, the weekly range is wider, and the replacement level is so low that a mediocre starter is worth more than the waiver wire's best week. If you drafted Sam LaPorta or anyone in that tier and the first two weeks have been uneven, you are not learning what you think you are learning. Two games of tight end data is close to no data at all.

Cut the position more rope than feels comfortable. The people who panic at tight end in September are reliably the people streaming it in November.

What to actually do this week

  1. Sort your roster by snap share, not by points. Look at the gap between the

two columns. That gap is your buy list and your sell list.

  1. Make one trade offer on a player whose usage is strong and whose box score

is not. That window closes the moment his numbers catch up.

  1. Leave your bench alone. Two weeks of a bench player is the single least

reliable data in the sport.

  1. Check the schedule, not the standings. Records this early are mostly a

record of who you played.

The teams that win leagues in September are not the ones that made the most moves. They are the ones that correctly did nothing four times and then moved hard on the fifth.
