Our story

Your hand knows
before you do.

We spent two years trying to put a wall between people and their phones. The math — and our own beta testers — taught us why that was the wrong shape of solution.

By [Founder Name], co-founder | January 2026 | 7 min read

I.  The reflex

Watch your own hand for one day. Not your screen time — your hand. You will catch it moving toward your pocket in the four seconds between sitting down and the meeting starting. On the walk from the desk to the kitchen. At a red light. In the half-second lull in a conversation with someone you love.

The phone stopped being a tool somewhere along the way and became a reflex. A tool is something you decide to pick up. A reflex happens first, and your reasons get written afterward — I was checking the time, I was expecting a message, I was just seeing if. By the time the sentence forms, the screen is already lit.

That distinction is the whole thing. You cannot reason your way out of a movement that starts before the reasoning does. Which is why advice like “be more present” lands the way “sleep better” lands at 3 a.m.: accurate, useless, faintly insulting.

The math nobody does

186
pickups a day for the average person. Not minutes — separate, discrete reaches for the same rectangle.
6 hours
At a conservative two minutes a pickup, that is over six hours of your day. Most people guess two, and are not lying — the time genuinely does not register.
43 hours
A week. A full-time job, worked every week, unpaid, spent staring at glass — with no promotion at the end of it.

Run that number against your own life and the arithmetic gets personal fast. Forty-three hours is a language. It is a draft of the book. It is every bedtime story your kid will remember. It is not that the time is wasted, exactly — it is that nobody ever agreed to spend it.

II.  Digital whack-a-mole

So you do the obvious thing. You delete Instagram. And for about four days it works beautifully — right up until you notice you have started refreshing LinkedIn, an app you have never once enjoyed, with the exact cadence you used to give Instagram.

Then it is the weather app, checked six times for a forecast that has not changed. Then a mobile game you downloaded for a flight in 2023. Then the news, then your email, then — and this one is almost funny — your own camera roll, scrolled backward like a feed.

This is the part people blame themselves for, and shouldn't. The habit was never loyal to the app. It was loyal to the loop: a flicker of boredom, a reach, a variable reward, a return to boredom slightly faster than before. The icon on the home screen is interchangeable. The loop is the product.

Which means every solution built around removing a thing is playing the carnival game. You hit one mole, another pops up two feet to the left, and the machine keeps the quarter either way.

III.  What we built first

Our first answer was a physical one. We called it Shuttr — a phone case with a mechanical shutter, a small steel panel you had to deliberately slide open with your thumb before the screen was any use to you. The idea was to reinstall the pause that the reflex had deleted. One second of friction, one moment where a decision could fit.

We loved it. Honestly, we still do; there is a box of them in the office. And it worked — for about nine days. We shipped it to 140 testers and asked them every evening. Week one, screen time fell by a third. Week two, it was halfway back. By week four most people had stopped sliding the shutter closed at all, and a few had done something we found genuinely clarifying: they left it open permanently, like a drawbridge nobody bothers to raise.

So we iterated. A charging dock with a timed lock — defeated by one tester with a butter knife, and by another with the purchase of a second phone. A software-assisted Shuttr, very clever, very well reviewed by us. Every time, the same curve: a sharp drop, a slow return, a quiet abandonment. Nobody failed. The design did.

The uncomfortable part

We tried to build a physical solution to get people off their screens, but we realized something uncomfortable: no single product can cure an attention monopoly.

People don't just need a barrier. They need a framework. You cannot hardware-engineer your way out of a psychological ecosystem — not when the other side ships updates weekly, runs experiments continuously, and uses your own behaviour as the training data.

Four things four prototypes taught us

  1. 01

    Friction decays. Any obstacle you can defeat the same way twice stops being an obstacle. Habits route around a static barrier in roughly two weeks.

  2. 02

    Removal needs replacement. Every hour you subtract has to be spent somewhere. Detoxes that skip this step fail quietly, then blame the person.

  3. 03

    The trigger beats the app. Deleting one app moves the behaviour next door. Disarming the trigger — the notification, the unlock reflex, the bedside charger — moves all of them at once.

  4. 04

    Nobody is loyal to a tool. They are loyal to a result. Which is ultimately why we stopped selling ours.

IV.  What we're building now

So we went agnostic. No device to defend, no engagement metric to protect, no sponsorships from the apps we write about — which buys us the one thing a hardware company can never afford to say: sometimes the best answer is a free setting you already own, and it will take you four minutes.

What we make now is two things, and they are designed to work together.

The journalism

Teach you how it works

Deep-dive reporting on the mechanics of a single attention trap — the psychology, the business model, the specific design decision — written the way a financial journalist would explain a market. You cannot disarm a loop you cannot see.

The toolkit

Hand you what to do

The actual digital tools to execute it: the settings, the scripts, the swaps, the audits — each tested, each specific, each runnable the same evening you read about it. Ours when ours is best, somebody else's when theirs is.

A framework instead of a wall. Over a few months it compounds into the thing a product never gave our testers: a way of thinking they can adapt themselves, in January and again in June, when the platforms inevitably change the rules.

We still lend out the Shuttr prototypes sometimes. They do work — for about nine days. The difference is that now we can also tell you what to do on day ten.
